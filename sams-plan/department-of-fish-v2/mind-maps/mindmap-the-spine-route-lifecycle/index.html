Five stages, three domains, one question each

Why the top layer matters

The three domains are not decoration over the five stages. They are the fastest cut you can make, because each domain has a different owner, a different table, and a different class of fix. Deciding “this is a Domain B problem” in the first minute eliminates two thirds of the search space before you have run a single command.

Stages inside a domain share evidence. Domains do not. That is the whole trick.

Reading the domains in the right direction

Faults travel downhill. A Domain A problem shows up as a Domain C symptom — the user says “the app is broken,” which is stage 5 language for something that may have died at stage 1. So the symptom always arrives at the bottom and the cause can be anywhere above it.

That is why the ladder below runs top down rather than following the complaint. You start at “is it here at all,” because a yes at stage 1 is cheap to get and eliminates the entire control plane in one command.

Stage Detail

What happens, what it looks like when it breaks

Each card: the mechanism, the failure signature, and the one piece of evidence that settles it.

Stage 1 · Learned

The route arrives. BGP: a peer sent it. OSPF: an LSA is in the database. Static: someone typed it. Connected: the interface is up. None of your policy has run yet, so if it is not here the problem is upstream or at the adjacency — the peer is down, the neighbour never advertised it, or the sender's own outbound filter dropped it.

Failure signature: nothing to look at. An absence, which is the hardest thing to notice.

Stage 2 · Filtered (accepted)

Inbound policy runs and decides what is allowed into the protocol table. A route can be received and not accepted. Those are two different words in the documentation and two different states on the box — and EP-15 exists because of the gap between them. Without soft-reconfiguration inbound the pre-policy view is not even stored, so you cannot see what you rejected.

Failure signature: your side is silent, their side swears they are advertising it. Both are telling the truth.

Stage 3 · Chosen (best path)

Several paths to the same prefix now compete within one protocol. Two things matter more than the tiebreak list itself. First, only the winner is offered upward — everything else sits in the database, valid but unused. That is design, not fault; multipath is what changes it. Second, next-hop reachability is a gate, not a tiebreak: a BGP path whose next hop cannot be resolved is invalid and never competes at all.

Failure signature: present and correct, just not the path you wanted. Traffic goes somewhere reasonable, so nobody raises a ticket for weeks.

Stage 4 · Installed (into the FIB)

Each protocol's winner now competes across protocols for a slot in the forwarding table, and the referee is administrative distance. Lowest distance wins regardless of how good the path actually is. This is the only stage where OSPF, BGP and a static route argue with each other — and where a leftover static quietly beats a perfectly healthy BGP path with everything above reading green.

Failure signature: the routing table database and the routing table disagree.

Stage 5 · Forwarded on

The twist that makes this a FortiGate course rather than a routing course: the FIB is an input to the forwarding decision, not the decision itself. Policy routes and SD-WAN rules sit above it. An established session keeps its original decision until something re-evaluates it. Traffic the FortiGate generates itself takes its own path.

Failure signature: perfect routing, wrong path. The most demoralising fault there is — unless you know this stage exists.

The one-line version

Exists → allowed in → won its protocol → won across protocols → actually used. Five yeses and the packet leaves the right port. One no, anywhere, and the symptom looks identical from the user's desk.

At The Desk

The five-question ladder

Ask in this order. Stop at the first no. Change nothing until a stage has a name.

1
Is it here at all?
Domain A. Check the adjacency first, then whether the prefix was ever received. get router info bgp summary for peer state and prefix counts; get router info ospf neighbor for OSPF.
No → the fault is upstream or at the adjacency. Nothing on this box will fix it.
2
Did my policy let it in?
Domain A. Compare received against accepted. get router info bgp neighbors <ip> received-routes versus get router info bgp neighbors <ip> routes.
No → your inbound prefix list, route map or max-prefix limit. Yours to fix.
3
Did it win inside its own protocol?
Domain B. Look at the candidates, not the winner. get router info bgp network <prefix> shows every path and marks the best; get router info routing-table database shows candidates the FIB never saw. Check next-hop resolution before blaming attributes.
No → attributes, metrics, or an unresolvable next hop. Not a filtering problem.
4
Did it win against the other protocols?
Domain B. Compare the database with the installed table. get router info routing-table database versus get router info routing-table all. If a route is in the first and not the second, you are looking at administrative distance.
No → a lower-distance source exists. Look for statics before anything else.
5
Is anything above the FIB overriding it?
Domain C. diagnose firewall proute list for policy routes and their hit counts; diagnose sys sdwan service for which member a rule actually selected; the session table for what an existing flow is still doing.
Yes → the routing is fine. Stop reading route output.
The discipline

Pick the command that answers the one question you are on — not the command you always run. Configuration changes come after the stage is named, never before. That habit is most of what separates an architect from a very fast administrator.

The Toolbox

One command per question

Grouped by stage, so the command follows the model instead of the other way round.

 Domain A — does it exist here?
# Stage 1 — learned
get router info bgp summary                        # peer state + prefixes received
get router info ospf neighbor                      # adjacency state (Full?)
get router info ospf database                      # is the LSA even here
get router info bgp neighbors <ip> received-routes  # BEFORE inbound policy

# Stage 2 — filtered / accepted
get router info bgp neighbors <ip> routes           # AFTER inbound policy
get router info bgp neighbors <ip> advertised-routes# what we send them
received-routes needs: config router bgp > config neighbor > set soft-reconfiguration enable
 Domain B — did it win?
# Stage 3 — chosen (inside one protocol)
get router info bgp network <prefix>     # every candidate path, best marked
get router info routing-table database   # candidates, including ones that lost
get router info ospf route               # OSPF's own view after SPF

# Stage 4 — installed (across protocols)
get router info routing-table all        # what actually won
get router info routing-table details <prefix>
get router info kernel                   # the FIB itself
database has it, "all" does not  =  administrative distance
 Domain C — is it actually used?
# Stage 5 — forwarded on
diagnose firewall proute list            # policy routes + hit counts + last hit
diagnose sys sdwan service               # which member each rule selected
diagnose sys sdwan member                # member state feeding that choice
diagnose sys session filter dst <ip>
diagnose sys session list                 # the decision an existing flow is still using
correct FIB + wrong egress = look here, not at route output

Administrative distance — the stage 4 referee

FortiOS defaults. The one that catches people coming from other vendors is the static route: 10, not 1 — still low enough to beat every dynamic protocol, which is exactly why a forgotten static is the classic stage 4 fault.

SourceDefault distanceBeats
Directly connected0Everything
Static10Every dynamic protocol on the box
eBGP20OSPF, RIP, iBGP
OSPF110RIP, iBGP
RIP120iBGP
iBGP200Nothing else by default
Distance vs metric

Metric decides stage 3 — which path wins inside a protocol. Distance decides stage 4 — which protocol wins across protocols. They never compete with each other, and a route with a beautiful metric loses to a worse route with a lower distance every time.

If two routes reach stage 4 with the same distance, FortiOS uses priority to break the tie — lower is preferred.

Exam & Field Traps

The places the model gets misread

Each of these is a stage boundary that looks like it is somewhere else.

Trap 1 · “The peer is up, so the route arrived”

An Established session proves the adjacency, not the advertisement. Stage 1 and stage 2 are separate yeses. The prefix count in the summary output is the cheapest way to tell them apart.

Trap 2 · “It's in the BGP table, so it's in the routing table”

Three things still have to happen: it must win inside BGP, its next hop must resolve, and it must beat every other protocol's candidate on distance. Received is not accepted; accepted is not selected; selected is not always installed.

Trap 3 · “Only one path is shown, so only one path exists”

Single best path is the default. The losers are still in the database, valid and ready. Multipath is what puts more than one in the FIB — and without it, a second path you can see in the protocol table does nothing for you during a failure until a timer expires.

Trap 4 · “Unreachable next hop just means a worse path”

No — it means no path. Reachability is a gate at stage 3, not a tiebreak. And when a recursive next hop stops resolving, the prefix is marked unresolvable and leaves the FIB entirely, so traffic falls through to the default route rather than blackholing cleanly. That is a much noisier failure than a clean drop.

Trap 5 · “The routing table is right, so the path is right”

Stage 5 exists precisely because this is false on a FortiGate. Policy routes and SD-WAN rules sit above the FIB, and the session table remembers yesterday's answer. Perfect routing with the wrong egress is normal, diagnosable, and lives in a completely different set of commands.

Carry-in from Season 2

Two items land directly on this model. Recursive next-hop resolution is a stage 3 gate — it is why a valid-looking BGP path can contribute nothing. Administrative distance and FIB installation is stage 4, and it is taught first in EP-13 because everything else on this page stacks on it.

Self-Test

The Flip-Card Wall

Name the stage out loud before you flip. A correct answer after peeking doesn't count — house rules.

Domains
What is the single fastest cut you can make in the first minute of a routing fault?
tap / click to flip

Name the domain, not the fault. Does it exist here (A), did it win (B), or is it actually used (C)? Each domain has a different owner, a different table and a different class of fix, so picking one eliminates two thirds of the search space before you run a command.

Stage 1 vs 2
The BGP peer is Established and the neighbour insists they are advertising 10.50.0.0/16. You see nothing. Which stage, and what proves it?
tap / click to flip

Stage 2 is the suspect, stage 1 is the test. Compare received-routes against routes for that neighbour. If it appears in received but not in routes, your inbound policy dropped it. If it appears in neither, you are back at stage 1 and the fault is theirs.

Stage 2
Why might received-routes return nothing even when routes genuinely arrived?
tap / click to flip

The pre-policy view is not stored by default. Without set soft-reconfiguration enable on the neighbour, the FortiGate discards what it rejected, so you cannot see what your own filter removed. Enable it before you conclude the neighbour is silent.

Stage 3
Is next-hop reachability a tiebreak or a gate? Why does the distinction matter?
tap / click to flip

A gate. A path whose next hop cannot be resolved is invalid and never enters the comparison at all — it does not simply lose. So a path that looks present, correct and well-attributed can be contributing nothing, and no amount of attribute tuning will change that.

Stage 3
Two paths, identical in every respect, and only one is in the routing table. Fault or design?
tap / click to flip

Design. Single best path is the default; the loser stays in the database, valid but unused. Multipath is what puts both in the FIB. The operational consequence is the important half: without it, the second path does nothing during a failure until a timer expires.

Stage 4
The prefix is in routing-table database but not in routing-table all. What have you just proved?
tap / click to flip

It won stage 3 and lost stage 4. Something else holds that prefix at a lower administrative distance. Look for a static first — on FortiOS its default distance is 10, low enough to beat every dynamic protocol on the box.

Stage 4
Metric and administrative distance — what does each one decide?
tap / click to flip

Metric decides inside a protocol; distance decides between protocols. Stage 3 versus stage 4. They never compete, and a route with an excellent metric loses to a worse route from a lower-distance source every time. Equal distance is then broken by priority, lower preferred.

Stage 5
Routing is verified correct and traffic still leaves the wrong interface. Where do you look, and where do you stop looking?
tap / click to flip

Stop reading route output. The FIB is an input to the forwarding decision, not the decision. Check the policy route table, then SD-WAN service selection, then the session table for a cached earlier decision. Perfect routing with the wrong egress is a normal FortiGate outcome.

Cascade
A recursive next hop stops resolving. Does traffic blackhole?
tap / click to flip

No — worse. The prefix is marked unresolvable and leaves the FIB, so traffic falls through to whatever is left, typically the default route, and fails somewhere further along. A clean drop would be easier to diagnose than the wrong-path failure you actually get.

Discipline
What is the rule about when configuration changes are allowed?
tap / click to flip
