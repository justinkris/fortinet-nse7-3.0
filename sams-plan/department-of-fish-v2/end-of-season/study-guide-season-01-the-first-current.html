SEASON STORY PROGRESSION

How the Department Got Here

Season 1 opens with the Department of Fish running a functional but immature network: Telstra Tower as HQ, Tuggers as primary data centre, Gunners as secondary, fibre between them, and a basic two-member SD-WAN deployment at Tuggers with zero explicit rules. Every episode's business problem came directly from a gap in that starting design — nothing was manufactured for the sake of teaching a topic.

EP-01 — The Two Roads Out of Tuggers

Executive-floor video stutters inconsistently despite fast downloads. Root cause: zero explicit SD-WAN rules — all traffic, including Teams, was governed by the capacity- and quality-blind implicit rule. Fix: first explicit rule (Teams/ISDB, Lowest Cost SLA).

EP-02 — Up Is Not Good

Six days later, Teams breaks up again despite the dashboard showing both members "Alive." Root cause: port1 genuinely degraded (SLA-failing) but still reachable; the new rule was correctly avoiding it for new sessions — the real cause was session stickiness, an unexplained mechanism at the time.

EP-03 — The First Packet Chooses

No new incident — EP-02's mechanism gets its explanation. SD-WAN rule lookup happens once, at session birth; forwarding info is cached and reused. Only a manual clear or genuine FIB/route change forces re-evaluation — never an SLA verdict alone.

EP-04 — The Implicit Rule

Racks reopens the implicit rule nobody has re-examined since EP-01. Both members are healthy, but source-ip-hash load-balancing is blind to the 500:200 capacity ratio, allowing proportional saturation of the smaller link even under perfect conditions. Fix proposed (not yet applied): weight-based load-balance-mode, 5:2 ratio.

EP-05 — Who Wins?

A genuine, scheduled DR test is booked — Tuggers goes fully offline for 40 minutes. Before approving it, Samwise proves the sla_map bit-order cold and reasons through all four SD-WAN strategies (Manual, Best Quality, Lowest Cost, Load Balance) so the team can predict exactly what will happen to live Teams sessions.

EP-06 — Direct to the Internet

The DR test succeeds exactly as predicted. Immediately after, the Department's first satellite office (Sydney) surfaces a new problem: SaaS traffic backhauling to Tuggers before reaching the internet. Season closes with a hybrid DIA breakout design and a self-predicted preview of Season 2's central-management problem.

TECHNOLOGY BLOCK 1 — SD-WAN FOUNDATIONS

Member, Health, and the Alive vs Good Split (EP-01/EP-02)

A member is a physical or logical WAN interface participating in an SD-WAN zone — the zone decouples policy from physical interfaces. A health check is a continuous probe that yields three raw measurements every round trip: latency, jitter (variance between round trips), and packet loss. An SLA target is a threshold layered on top of that same measurement — the health check doesn't change depending on whether a target is configured.

Core distinction: Alive = the probe returned within timeout (a reachability fact). Good = the measured values are within configured SLA thresholds (a compliance verdict). A member can be Alive and simultaneously fail every SLA target.

sla_map

A bitmask, LSB-first, one bit per configured SLA target within a single member's health check (not one bit per member). 1 = target met, 0 = target missed. E.g. sla_map=0x5 (binary 101) = target 1 met, target 2 missed, target 3 met.

Strategy filtering behaviour

StrategySLA-aware?Selection logic
ManualNo — completely SLA-blindFirst Alive member in configured preference order, regardless of SLA compliance.
Lowest Cost (SLA)YesSLA-compliant members form the eligible pool (demoted, not removed, if all fail — see EP-03 refinement below); lowest static cost wins among survivors.

MENTAL NOTE
Up is a fact. Good is a measurement against a threshold. A rule can be steering correctly for new sessions while an old, already-established session quietly rides a degraded link — and that's not a rule bug, it's a session-lifecycle question (EP-03's territory).
TECHNOLOGY BLOCK 2 — SESSIONS & RULE ORDERING

First Packet Chooses, Then the Access Card Rules (EP-03/EP-04)

The front desk / access card model: the SD-WAN rule table (inspection plane) is consulted exactly once, at session birth. The resulting interface + next-hop is cached in the session (forwarding plane) and reused for every subsequent packet — no re-lookup, no matter how badly the member's SLA status changes afterward.

The only two re-evaluation triggers

  1. Manual/admin session clear — an explicit administrative action.
  2. Genuine FIB/route change — interface down, routing protocol neighbor lost, route withdrawn from the FIB.
Non-trigger, tested repeatedly this season: an SLA/health-check verdict alone — however severe (sla_map=0x0 included) — never forces re-evaluation of an already-established session. This is purely an inspection-plane event.

Refresh-in-place vs. tear-down-and-rebuild: when a genuine FIB/route change occurs, the existing session's forwarding info is normally refreshed in place (same 5-tuple, session identity persists) — evidenced by a continuous session duration/age counter, not the SD-WAN member/interface field (which is expected to change either way). Note: this is not universal — SNAT-translated sessions may behave differently (deferred to EP-22).

The asymmetry (EP-05): a route disappearing IS a trigger; a route returning is NOT. There is no "did something better just appear" trigger. This produces gradual, session-by-session trickle-back after a link recovers, gated entirely by each session's own natural end (TCP RST/FIN, UDP idle timeout, or manual clear) — never a bulk cutover.

The implicit rule is not a "dumb default"

It uses the identical session-birth-then-cache mechanism as any explicit rule, just governed by zone-level load-balance-mode instead of a rule's strategy field. Eligibility requires Alive AND a valid FIB route — health-check status alone is not sufficient.

load-balance-modeBasisBest for
source-ip-based (default)Session-count fairness (hash)Nothing specific — blind to both capacity and SLA
weight-basedStatic, admin-configured ratioFixed, known, permanent capacity differences (e.g. 500:200 → 5:2)
usage-basedDynamic, live-measured throughputFluctuating capacity/conditions
Architecture nuance: explicit-rule strategies (Manual/Best Quality/Lowest Cost/Maximize Bandwidth) and load-balance-mode settings are mutually exclusive tool sets. An explicit catch-all rule cannot substitute for weight-based/usage-based distribution — it loses access to that capacity-aware mechanism entirely.
TECHNOLOGY BLOCK 3 — STRATEGY MECHANICS

Two-Stage Decisions: Filter, Then Tiebreak (EP-05)

Best Quality SLA and Lowest Cost SLA share an identical two-stage structure:

  1. Stage 1 — SLA compliance filters the eligible pool. A member failing its configured target(s) is excluded from ordinary consideration by either strategy.
  2. Stage 2 — the configured tiebreaker decides among survivors. Best Quality: exactly one configured link-cost-factor metric (latency, jitter, packet-loss, or a named composite option) — never a live blended score. Lowest Cost: each member's static, admin-assigned cost value.
The one exception: Lowest Cost's total-failure fallback. If every eligible member fails every configured SLA target simultaneously, Stage 1 is skipped entirely and cost alone decides among all members, compliance disregarded.

sla_map vs. health-check — two different questions: sla_map is a per-session, per-member snapshot, frozen at the moment the owning explicit rule last evaluated — it says nothing about any other member. The live, all-member, real-time view is a separate command entirely: diagnose sys sdwan health-check.

⚠ Needs Reinforcement

The two-stage filter-then-tiebreak structure was missed identically on both Best Quality and Lowest Cost framings in the same exam set (treating it as single-stage) — corrected both times via self-restatement, but flagged as the season's clearest single reinforcement item, later reconfirmed on unrelated content in EP-06.

TECHNOLOGY BLOCK 4 — DIA TOPOLOGIES

Central, Local, and Hybrid Breakout (EP-06)

ShapePathVisibilityBest for
Central breakoutBranch → DC → InternetSingle point (DC firewall)Whitelisted IPs (known SNAT), heavy DC-side inspection
Local breakoutBranch → Internet directlyBranch FGT inspects + forwards logs to FortiAnalyzerSaaS (Teams, SharePoint) — nearest regional PoP
Hybrid breakoutBoth, chosen per SD-WAN rule (ISDB match)Split — local FGT+FAZ for local, DC firewall for centralReal-world Department design

The core architectural insight: backhauling doesn't just add hops — it forces traffic to whatever regional point of presence is nearest the DC, not the branch, meaning the branch never reaches its own nearest copy of the SaaS service.

Remember: local breakout ≠ loss of visibility. The source of inspection/logging moves from the DC firewall to the branch firewall; FortiAnalyzer still receives the logs either way.

Building a hybrid design requires a second SD-WAN member at the branch (the local internet circuit, typically its own zone) before any rule can select it — the rule chooses between members, not directly between routes.

Scaling this design across many branches by hand creates policy drift — a self-predicted, correct preview of Season 2's FortiManager/template content.

KEY DECISION ORDERS

Where Each Decision Actually Gets Made

  • New session path selection: SD-WAN rule table (top-down, first match) → implicit rule fallback (load-balance-mode + FIB) if no explicit match.
  • Existing session forwarding: cached at birth; re-evaluated only by manual clear or FIB/route change — never by SLA verdict alone, and never by rule table reordering/insertion.
  • Best Quality / Lowest Cost SLA: SLA compliance filters first; tiebreaker (link-cost-factor / static cost) decides among survivors; total SLA failure is the sole exception for Lowest Cost.
  • Hybrid DIA breakout: member/zone must exist at the branch before a rule can select it; the rule then discriminates by destination/application (ISDB), not by "is this branch local or central" as an all-or-nothing choice.
  • Explicit rule fails to match → falls through to the next explicit rule, or ultimately the implicit rule (still FIB-based, no ISDB/SLA awareness).
TROUBLESHOOTING WORKFLOWS

Plane-First Discipline

Every troubleshooting scenario this season followed the same order: isolate which plane the symptom belongs to before touching configuration.

  1. Forwarding plane first: which member did the session actually select? Is that member currently Alive/SLA-compliant? (diagnose sys sdwan health-check, diagnose firewall session list/diagnose sys session list).
  2. Only then, inspection plane: policy, profile, or DC-side inspection — checked after forwarding-plane evidence rules out a routing/member cause.
  3. Session age/duration is the correct evidence for refresh-in-place vs. tear-down-and-rebuild — not the SD-WAN member field, which changes either way.
  4. Proportional saturation checks (EP-04) use diagnose sys sdwan member for measured throughput vs. configured bandwidth — not get system interface, which is administrative state only.
⚠ Needs Reinforcement

This exact ordering was skipped twice across the season under exam/scenario pressure — once jumping to "SLA changes existing sessions" (EP-03 exam item), and again jumping straight to "inspection problem" before checking the forwarding plane (EP-06). Both times self-corrected once redirected — worth a deliberate first-response check ("which plane is this?") before answering scenario questions in Season 2.

MISCONCEPTIONS CORRECTED THIS SEASON

Recurring Failure Patterns

  • sla_map "member vs. target" conflation (EP-02): corrected via bit-by-bit walkthrough — bits are per-target within one member, not per-member.
  • "Lowest Cost SLA deprioritizes, doesn't exclude" (EP-02): corrected — failing even one target excludes a member from the pool, refined further in EP-03 to "demotion within the same list," which matters only in the total-failure edge case.
  • "Periodic SLA re-check affects existing sessions" (EP-03 exam item): corrected — no periodic re-check; only manual clear or FIB change re-evaluates.
  • Implicit-rule eligibility reduced to health-check only (EP-04): corrected back to Alive AND valid FIB route.
  • "Lowest Cost always overrides SLA" / "link-cost-factor can blend metrics" (EP-05, Q3 & Q7): both corrected via self-restatement of the two-stage filter-then-tiebreak model.
  • "The prerequisite for a hybrid rule is the correct routes" (EP-06): corrected via scope reduction to "an SD-WAN member."
  • "Rule fails to match = traffic dies" (EP-06): corrected via the explicit-rule-fallback model — session falls through instead.
✓ Strong Area

Proactive self-disclosure of genuine uncertainty (EP-05's sla_map scope moment) rather than guessing forward — a consistently mature, senior-architect-level habit across the season.

INTEGRATED SCENARIO

Bringing It All Together

Scenario: Sydney's hybrid breakout is live. Teams is steered locally via ISDB; everything else goes to Tuggers via the DC-facing member. Tuggers then experiences a genuine FIB-level outage (as in EP-05's DR test).

Walk the full chain: Sydney's DC-facing member loses its route → existing sessions on that member are torn down (FIB/route-change trigger, EP-03) → new packets birth new sessions → those new sessions consult Sydney's SD-WAN rules fresh → whitelisted/central traffic re-evaluates against whatever DC-facing path remains (Gunners, if a secondary path exists) → Teams traffic is unaffected throughout, since it was never routed via Tuggers in the first place (EP-06's hybrid design isolates it from a DC-only outage). When Tuggers returns, sessions on Gunners do NOT snap back — they trickle back gradually as each session naturally ends (EP-05's asymmetry).

This scenario deliberately chains EP-03 (triggers), EP-05 (DR failover + trickle-back), and EP-06 (hybrid breakout isolation) into a single mental walk-through — exactly the kind of integrated reasoning the exam rewards.

SEASON REVIEW QUESTIONS

Ten Questions, Cold

1. What is the difference between a member being "Alive" and being "Good"?
Alive is a reachability fact (probe returned within timeout); Good is a compliance verdict (measured latency/jitter/loss within configured SLA thresholds). Independent facts.
2. Does an SLA verdict change ever force re-evaluation of an established session?
No — only a manual/admin clear or a genuine FIB/route change does. SLA status alone, however severe, never touches the cached forwarding entry.
3. What are the two conditions for implicit-rule member eligibility?
Alive (health-check) AND a valid FIB route. Neither alone is sufficient.
4. Why is weight-based load-balance-mode preferred over source-ip-based for a fixed 500:200 capacity split?
Source-ip-based distributes by session count, blind to capacity, allowing the smaller link to saturate proportionally faster. Weight-based applies a static, admin-set ratio matching the known, permanent capacity difference.
5. Describe the two-stage decision structure shared by Best Quality and Lowest Cost SLA.
Stage 1: SLA compliance filters the eligible pool. Stage 2: the configured tiebreaker (link-cost-factor / static cost) decides among survivors — except Lowest Cost's total-failure case, where Stage 1 is skipped and cost alone decides.
6. Why does a route returning NOT pull an established session back to it?
There is no "did something better just appear" trigger — only "did what I'm using just disappear." Recovery produces gradual trickle-back as each session naturally ends, not a bulk cutover.
7. What must exist at a branch FortiGate before a hybrid DIA rule can steer SaaS traffic locally?
A second SD-WAN member (the local internet circuit, typically its own zone) — the rule selects between members, not directly between routes.
8. Does local breakout mean the SOC loses visibility into that traffic?
No — the branch FortiGate inspects locally and forwards logs to FortiAnalyzer. The source of inspection moves; the visibility does not disappear.
9. If an explicit rule's target member fails health check with no alternate eligible member, what happens to that traffic?
The rule fails to match and the session falls through to the next explicit rule, or ultimately the implicit rule (still FIB-based) — the traffic is not dropped.
10. Why is relying on the implicit rule as an unplanned fallback risky for an application like Teams?
The implicit rule has no ISDB/application awareness (traffic is no longer identified as Teams) and no SLA awareness (a degraded-but-Alive member can be silently selected).
