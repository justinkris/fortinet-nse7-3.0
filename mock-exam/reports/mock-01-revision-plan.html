💭 THINK FIRST · RESULTS

A mock report gives you two independent signals: what you got wrong, and what you knew you might get wrong. Most candidates read only the first. The second is the one that predicts how you will behave under exam pressure, when there is no answer key and no second attempt.

  • Two candidates both score 75%. One flagged eight questions, the other flagged none. Which one is closer to passing, and why?
  • If a domain scores badly because you were never taught it, is that the same kind of problem as a domain that scores badly after four episodes of coverage?
Q1 — MODEL ANSWER
The one who flagged eight. Flagging means the internal boundary between "I know this" and "I am guessing" is intact, so their revision can be aimed and their exam-day time can be triaged. Flagging nothing while missing ten means the boundary is not being detected at all — every wrong answer was submitted with the same confidence as every right one. That candidate cannot self-audit during the exam, cannot decide what to revisit with the remaining time, and will not notice a domain slipping until the score report arrives.
Q2 — MODEL ANSWER
No, and conflating them wastes revision time. A domain never taught needs first exposure and mental-model construction — hours of new work. A domain taught but failing needs diagnosis: is it recall, is it a misconception, or is it a transfer failure into scenario wording? The remedies are completely different, and reading a flat score table cannot tell them apart. Cross-referencing against the episode history is what separates the two.

💭 THINK FIRST · PATTERNS

Ten wrong answers scattered across five sections looks like ten separate problems. It rarely is. Before reading the diagnosis, consider what a single underlying cause would have to look like to produce that spread.

  • Your BGP registers show weight and local preference solid, and the oldest-route tiebreaker reasoned out cleanly. So why would a two-IBGP-peer tie question still fail?
  • You have four episodes of session-behaviour coverage and a strong stated mental model. What kind of gap survives that and still costs you a mark?
Q1 — MODEL ANSWER
Because a tie between two IBGP peers with equal weight and equal local preference has already passed the two steps you own, and has to be resolved in the five steps you have never produced: locally originated, AS-path length, origin type, MED, EBGP over IBGP. You hold the top of the list and the bottom of the list. The question landed in the middle.
Q2 — MODEL ANSWER
A recall gap sitting on top of a sound model. Your registers are explicit that the CLI strings in this track — snat-route-change, preserve-session-route — were supplied by the mentor and never produced by you. Understanding why SNAT changes kill a session does not automatically hand you the keyword the exam asks for by name. Re-reading will not close this; typing it cold will.
SECTION 02

Five Patterns Across Ten Wrong Answers

Cross-referencing the misses against your session summaries and knowledge registers, the ten failures resolve into five causes — and only two of them are what most people would call "weak areas".

Pattern A
Confident-wrong, not uncertain-wrong

Zero flags on ten misses. The internal confidence signal is not tracking the actual knowledge boundary. Worth fixing independently of any content, because an exam you cannot self-audit while sitting it is more dangerous than one where you know exactly where the soft spots are.

Pattern B
Two whole clusters are untaught, not forgotten

EP-29 (FGCP Beyond the Basics) and EP-30 (FGSP and Asymmetry) were skipped — no session summary or knowledge register exists for either. That accounts for essentially all of the Section 5.0 loss. Separately, EP-35 and EP-36 have not happened yet, which accounts for essentially all of the Section 3.0 loss. The mock ran ahead of the syllabus.

Pattern C
BGP best-path: bookends but no middle

Weight and local preference are solid (EP-16, EP-33). The oldest-route incumbency tiebreaker was reasoned out well at EP-33. The five steps between have never been produced, tested or recited in any episode. Q23 sits precisely in that gap.

Pattern D
Conceptually solid, never typed cold

Session behaviour (EP-21, EP-22) and metadata variables (EP-09) both show strong conceptual understanding in the registers, but the command strings were supplied rather than produced. This is the cheapest class of gap to close — and the one least likely to close by itself.

Pattern E
Isolated misses inside strong tracks

MTU/MSS/FEC and FGCP primary election are largely sound — the full primary-selection order including the five-minute uptime threshold was produced cold at EP-34. The npu_flag byte-code lifecycle is the single item never drilled. One focused pass; do not over-invest.

Net read: roughly seven of the ten misses are syllabus gaps rather than retention failures. That is structurally good news — the remedy is to run the missing episodes, not to relearn material that has decayed.

SECTION 05

Twelve Days, In Order

Sequenced so the untaught episodes land before the consolidation days that depend on them, and so the recalibration day sits at the midpoint where it can still change how you approach the second half.

Day 01
BGP route selection — the full order

Reread the ten-step tie-breaker list (Enterprise Firewall guide, p.344). Write it from memory three times until error-free. Then explain in your own words why weight and local preference come before AS-path, and why router ID is last.

Day 02
Run EP-29 — FGCP Beyond the Basics

Active-active, virtual clustering, virtual MAC assignment logic, the three ethertypes, sync optimisation, VDOM partitioning. This closes the largest single Section 5.0 gap.

Day 03
Run EP-30 — FGSP and Asymmetry

Standalone sync and its defaults, coverage and limits, pickup options, L2 versus L3 sync, encrypted sync (L3 only), asymmetric session-owner forwarding, and the FGCP / FGSP / VRRP comparison.

Day 04
MTU, MSS, FEC and offload evidence

Drill the npu_flag lifecycle until each stage can be stated unprompted. Retype the FEC phase-1 block from memory — phase 1, not phase 2, is the examinable trap. Revisit MTU defaults and the MSS clamping trade-off.

Day 05
Metadata variables — light pass only

This track already rates strongly. Close the two soft spots: the orphaned-mapping timeline, and override-deletion versus reference-removal. Quick self-test, then stop.

Day 06
Session behaviour and failover — hands-on

Work the SD-WAN guide's SNAT / IP-pool / failback lab (pp. 284–301), or walk it cold on paper. Type — do not merely recognise — snat-route-change and preserve-session-route, and state what may_dirty, dirty and route_preserve each mean and which session class they govern.

Day 07
Recalibration day

Redo only the ten wrong questions from Mock 01. Before answering each one, explicitly flag anything you are not fully certain of. The point today is rebuilding the uncertainty signal from Pattern A — a correct answer that should have been flagged still counts as a miss.

Day 08
Run EP-35 — Multihub and Multiregion

Dual-hub options at scale, BGP self-healing, multiregion design guidelines (AS per region, IBGP within, EBGP between, summarise between regions), and VRF-aware overlays with vpn-id-ipip tagging.

Day 09
Run EP-36 — ADVPN 1.0 to 2.0

Shortcut negotiation and lifecycle, FortiManager deployment, IBGP/EBGP placement, placeholders, timeout and failback, dependent shortcuts, BGP on loopback, and the 1.0 versus 2.0 distinction.

Day 10
ADVPN and multiregion consolidation

Build a 1.0-versus-2.0 comparison table from memory: edge discovery, path management, transport groups, UDP 4684, IPv4-only support, fallback behaviour. Drill sender / receiver / forwarder roles, spoke net-device enable, and why add-route stays disabled under dynamic routing.

Day 11
Integrated review

Reread the new EP-29, EP-30, EP-35 and EP-36 knowledge registers alongside the BGP and offload notes. Then build yourself a 15–20 question set covering only the ten missed sub-topics, and sit it with no notes open.

Day 12
Fresh mock, timed

Full conditions, flagging as you go. Compare section scores directly against this baseline — Sections 3.0 and 5.0 should move furthest.

Pacing note: Mock 01 took 28:54 for 40 questions, which is comfortable. Speed is not your constraint. Resist the urge to compress this schedule to prove it.

SECTION 06

Readiness Checklist — Before You Book

Every item below is verifiable. None of them is "feel confident about".

  • Recite the full BGP best-path order — all ten steps, including the five middle ones — cold, twice in a row, without error.
  • EP-29 and EP-30 completed, with their knowledge registers showing at least three stars on FGCP-beyond-primary-election and on FGSP / VRRP / asymmetry.
  • EP-35 and EP-36 completed, with registers showing at least three stars on multiregion / VRF-aware overlays and on ADVPN 1.0 versus 2.0.
  • Type snat-route-change and preserve-session-route from memory and explain what each does, unprompted.
  • State the npu_flag lifecycle stages and what each one means, unprompted.
  • Re-attempt of Mock 01's ten wrong questions scores at least 8/10, with confidence flags placed accurately — this is the Pattern A close, and a lucky 10/10 with no flags does not pass it.
  • Sections 3.0 and 5.0 both score 75% or better on a fresh mock.
  • Overall fresh-mock score 80% or better, with no single section below 70%.
The one to watch: the flagging item. It is the only checklist entry that measures how you will behave in a room with no answer key, and it is the one this report says is currently missing entirely.
