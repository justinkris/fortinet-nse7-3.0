💭 THINK FIRST · STORY & PROBLEM

A carrier fault at Tuggers lasted eleven minutes and nothing broke. That is a good outcome and a bad piece of evidence — it proves the estate survives a link, and says nothing about surviving a site.

  • One of Perth's two overlays to Tuggers dropped. Why did the IBGP session not drop with it?
  • The session is built between loopbacks (10.200.1.21 ↔ 10.200.1.1). A loopback is not attached to any physical or tunnel interface, so it does not go down when a link does. Both TCP endpoints stayed up, so the session had no reason to reset — and no route event was generated.
Q1 — MODEL ANSWER
Everything kept working. What exactly had the design bought, and what had it not bought?
Q2 — MODEL ANSWER
It bought transport redundancy to a single hub. Every path Perth had still terminated at the same building. A site failure would have left four healthy tunnels pointing at nothing.
SECTION 01

Monday 02:14 — the failure that proved nothing

ISP1 into Tuggers suffered a carrier fault at 02:14 and recovered on its own at 02:25. Perth's overlay1 tunnel went with it. The compliance feed never stopped, the IBGP session never flapped, and nobody was paged.

Dr Finch's point was that this is the wrong reassurance. The Department has evidence that it survives a transport. It has no evidence at all about a site — and that is the sentence going into the quarterly risk paper.

Hub loss (hypothetical)Whole site, both transportsTotal, for internal appsUntested — no second hub exists
Business problem: a single hub means every branch's redundancy stops at one building. Commissioning Gunners as hub two is the only change that alters the risk statement.

An outage that causes no impact is not proof of resilience. It is proof of resilience at the layer that failed. Always ask which layer was tested.

💭 THINK FIRST · THREE LOOKUPS

A FortiGate cannot transmit toward an address — only out of an interface. That single constraint generates the whole forwarding model for an overlay, including why the design survives failures without moving the routing table.

  • Perth learns 10.10.0.0/16 with next hop 10.200.1.1, which is on none of its interfaces. What happens next?
  • A second route lookup — recursion. 10.200.1.1 matches the loopback-pool static route pointing at the overlay zone, which resolves to a set of tunnel members. Resolution completes only when a lookup lands on something with an interface.
Q1 — MODEL ANSWER
How many route lookups occur for one encapsulated packet, and which one involves the underlay?
Q2 — MODEL ANSWER
Three. (1) inner destination → next hop; (2) recursive next hop → overlay zone; (3) outer destination after encapsulation → physical port. Only the third involves the underlay, and SD-WAN sits between (2) and (3).
SECTION 02

Three lookups, three different destinations

The routing table entry for a hub-learned prefix names an address, not a door. That entry is unfinished, and the FortiGate finishes it by looking the address up again. That second lookup is recursion — not a feature, just another route lookup against a different destination.

The three address layers between Perth and Tuggers. Only the loopback layer is used as a BGP next hop.

        best match  10.200.0.0/24  (static, wizard-generated)

        resolves to overlay1, overlay2            -> COMPLETE

STEP 4  POLICY LOOKUP     LAN -> overlay zone, accept, no NAT

STEP 5  SD-WAN MEMBER SELECTION
        candidates  overlay1, overlay2     (tunnels, NOT ports)
        selected    overlay1

STEP 6  IPsec ENCAPSULATION
        outer src   port1 address       (from tunnel definition)
        outer dst   198.51.100.1        (from set remote-gw)

STEP 7  ROUTE LOOKUP #3   dst 198.51.100.1
        best match  0.0.0.0/0 via ISP1 gateway, port1
        SD-WAN plays no part -- the tunnel was bound to port1
        at build time, so choosing overlay1 already chose port1.

STEP 8  Session installed. All later packets take the fast path.

Steps 2 through 5 change nothing in the packet. The address that the whole recursion turns on — 10.200.1.1 — never appears on the wire at any point. It is a pointer inside the routing table.

Where the outer destination comes from: set remote-gw on the tunnel. It is static configuration, not something learned. Perth can hardcode it because the hub's public address is known in advance — the same asymmetry that makes the spoke the initiator and the hub the dial-up server.

What changes when a transport fails

 ISP1 upISP1 down
Lookup #110.10.0.0/16 → 10.200.1.1Unchanged
Lookup #210.200.1.1 → zone {overlay1, overlay2}10.200.1.1 → zone {overlay2}
SD-WANselects overlay1selects overlay2
BGP—No event. Endpoints unchanged.
Common mistake: writing the recursive lookup as the far-end tunnel address (10.202.1.1). The recursion target is always the loopback (10.200.1.1). A tunnel address is the far end of a point-to-point interface; nothing ever looks it up as a next hop.

When recursion fails entirely

If Tuggers is gone and the recursion cannot resolve, the prefix is marked unresolvable: it stays in the BGP RIB and is removed from the FIB. Lookup #1 then re-runs against what is left and finds the default route. Internal traffic is handed to the internet with an RFC1918 destination and dies at the policy lookup or is NAT'd into nothing.

Exam trap: an unresolvable recursive route does not blackhole cleanly. It falls through to the next-best match. No error, no alarm — traffic silently going somewhere useless.
🧠
MENTAL NOTE
The routing table does not move when a transport fails. Lookup #1 is unchanged and lookup #2 resolves to a smaller member set. That invariance is the product you buy with BGP on loopback.

💭 THINK FIRST · SECOND HUB

Adding a hub is arithmetic before it is configuration. Get the address plan wrong and the failure arrives years later, at the exact moment nobody wants to renumber thirty spokes.

  • Tuggers has two overlays. Gunners joins as hub two. How many overlay subnets does the estate need, and why?
  • Four. An overlay is a hub-transport PAIR, and a tunnel to Gunners terminates on Gunners' own dial-up interface, so it cannot share a subnet with Tuggers'. 10.201/10.202 for Tuggers, 10.203/10.204 for Gunners — all inside the reserved 10.200.0.0/14.
Q1 — MODEL ANSWER
Does Gunners need its own loopback pool?
Q2 — MODEL ANSWER
No. There is ONE pool estate-wide and Gunners takes one address from it (10.200.1.2). The pool identifies nodes, not hubs, and has nothing to do with transports — which is why it does not multiply when the overlays do.
SECTION 03

Gunners joins — two overlays become four

An overlay is a hub-transport pair. A second hub does not join the existing overlays; it creates its own set. Perth goes from two tunnels to four, on the same two physical ports, and from one IBGP session to two.

OVERLAY SUBNETS          scale with  hubs x transports
  10.201.1.0/24   overlay1   Tuggers - ISP1
  10.202.1.0/24   overlay2   Tuggers - ISP2
  10.203.1.0/24   overlay3   Gunners - ISP1     <- new

  ...           one per SD-WAN node, must be unique

Two overlays became four. Five nodes became six. The two pools scale on different axes and sizing each one for the wrong axis is the commonest planning error.

Where the formula breaks

hubs × transports is a shortcut, valid only when every hub carries every transport. An overlay exists only where both ends have the transport.

💭 THINK FIRST · HUB PREFERENCE

Two hubs are not automatically two options. Which hub carries traffic is decided in two places, and a design that assumes one layer can do the other's job installs cleanly and fails silently.

  • Tuggers should be primary and Gunners the fallback. Enforce it at the BGP layer or the SD-WAN layer — and what breaks if you pick the other?
  • SD-WAN, because it selects on measured SLA and can differentiate per application — it sees brownouts, not just blackouts, and BGP is blind to loss and latency. But choosing SD-WAN means configuring BGP to expose both hubs (ibgp-multipath) or the rule has no Gunners member to choose.
Q1 — MODEL ANSWER
ibgp-multipath is missing. All four tunnels are up and both BGP sessions are established. Tuggers dies. What happens?
Q2 — MODEL ANSWER
Traffic stops. The FIB holds only 10.200.1.1, so overlay3/overlay4 are never candidates for that prefix. The route becomes unresolvable and falls to the default route. Recovery waits on the IBGP hold timer expiring (default 180s) before the Gunners path is even considered.
SECTION 04

Which hub carries the traffic — and which layer decides

The rule: BGP decides which hubs are reachable. SD-WAN decides which tunnel is used. Neither can do the other's job.
 BGP layerSD-WAN layer
Failover triggerSession or route lossSLA breach
SeesBlackouts onlyBrownouts — latency, jitter, loss
GranularityAll traffic, one preferencePer rule, per application
SpeedHold timer / BFDSub-second member reselection
Role in this designMake both hubs reachableExpress the preference
The dependency that makes it work
# Default FortiGate behaviour installs ONE best path.
# Without this, the Gunners next hop never enters the FIB.

config router bgp
    set ibgp-multipath enable
end

  FIB WITHOUT multipath          FIB WITH multipath
  ----------------------         ---------------------
  10.10.0.0/16                   10.10.0.0/16
    -> 10.200.1.1                  -> 10.200.1.1
                                   -> 10.200.1.2

  recursion -> ov1, ov2          recursion -> ov1, ov2, ov3, ov4
  SD-WAN picks a TRANSPORT       SD-WAN picks a TRANSPORT and a HUB

Department design as installed: overlay1 / overlay2 as priority members, overlay3 / overlay4 as fallback, with ibgp-multipath enable underneath so the rule has four real candidates.

Most dangerous misconception of the episode — Needs Reinforcement: that SD-WAN can fail over to a second hub whose next hop is not in the FIB. It cannot. Four tunnels up, two sessions established, SLA green on every member, and the site is single-homed for forwarding until a hold timer expires. It survives design review, survives Install Preview, and only appears during the outage it was built to prevent.
Tiebreak trap: if two paths are genuinely identical (same AS, local pref, MED, origin), FortiGate falls to the final tiebreaker — typically lowest router-ID. That is a silent hub preference nobody designed.
🧠
MENTAL NOTE
Failover happens at the layer that has candidates. SD-WAN can only choose among members the FIB has already resolved.

💭 THINK FIRST · SYDNEY INCIDENT

Sydney's internet breakout disappeared on a Friday morning. Department traffic was fine, four tunnels were up, BGP was healthy, and nobody had touched the device since October. The season's whole argument is contained in that sentence.

  • The device did not change but its behaviour did. What class of cause does that leave, and what actually removed port5?
  • Authorship changed. Thursday's install wrote system.sdwan in full from SD-WAN_Branch-Standard. Port5 was absent from the template, and inside an authored block absence is deletion. There was no delete instruction and no error — the install reported success.
Q1 — MODEL ANSWER
Sydney survived nine months and two previous installs. What changed on the Thursday?
Q2 — MODEL ANSWER
Re-running the overlay orchestrator for dual hub regrouped and reassigned its own output across the device group, and Sydney picked up the SD-WAN template for the first time. Nobody assigned it deliberately. Hand-edit safety is a property of the device's current assignment set, and that set changed without anyone touching the device.

Why Install Preview did not catch it

  • Preview is per-device. A 29-device group install is 29 diffs. Nobody reads 29 diffs, so people sample — and sampling assumes the devices are alike.
  • The sample was on the wrong axis. Mel previewed the three devices the change affected most. The device at risk was the one the change did not affect; it was at risk because of what was already on it.
  • The diff shows absence, not deletion. There is no line reading “removing port5”. You have to notice what is not there.

The three copies, and where the install gap actually is

Assign / evaluate / install
TEMPLATE            the authored intent, shared
      |
      |  ASSIGN + EVALUATE   <- writes the device DB IMMEDIATELY,
      |                         no install, no warning
      v
DEVICE DB           per-device resolved config, held on FortiManager
      |
      |  INSTALL DEVICE SETTINGS   <- the only step that leaves FMG
      v
RUNNING CONFIG      the actual FortiGate

Install Preview = diff( device DB , running config )
                  NOT diff( template , device )
Exam trap: editing an assigned template changes the device DB for every assigned device — silently, immediately, with no install. The gap is always device DB → firewall, never template → device DB.

Finding the next one before it finds you

Config StatusMeaningDirectionAction
SynchronizedDevice DB matches running config—Safe to install
ModifiedFortiManager changed; firewall not yet toldFMG → FGT pendingPreview, then install
Out-of-SyncFirewall changed outside FortiManagerSomeone used the CLIInvestigate before installing

Sydney would have read Out-of-Sync from October onward — nine months of warning in a column nobody was reading. Retrieve Config pulls the drift into the device DB and the revision diff shows what changed outside FortiManager.

Limit of Retrieve: it makes drift visible and diffable. It does not reconcile the drift with the template — the next evaluation will overwrite it again. Retrieve tells you what to decide about; it does not decide.
Fix as installed: port5 and the Sydney-Breakout service moved into SD-WAN_Branch-Standard with an installation target of Sydney — template ownership plus scope, the same mechanism protecting Brisbane's MPLS member. Standing rule adopted: no install runs while any target device reads Out-of-Sync.

💭 THINK FIRST · EXAM & TRAPS

Three items from this episode were not answered independently and are the ones most likely to be tested. They are marked below.

  • A BGP route is present, the session is established and both tunnels are up — but it is not forwarding. Why?
  • It has been beaten into the FIB by a route with a lower administrative distance — almost always a leftover static. SD-WAN cannot prevent a route entering the FIB; it selects a member afterwards. Verify with get router info routing-table details (winner shows best) and get router info bgp network.
Q1 — MODEL ANSWER
State the general rule for what an install can and cannot overwrite.
Q2 — MODEL ANSWER
An install overwrites every config block that an assigned template authors — completely. Anything outside those blocks survives untouched. The unit of overwrite is the block, not the setting, so absence inside an authored block is deletion.

Needs reinforcement

Route selection into the FIB. Administrative distance, competing routes, and the RIB/FIB boundary were not reasoned about at all this session — both attempts landed in the wrong layer. This is the floor under Season 3 and is being taught explicitly in EP-13.
The failover timeline without ibgp-multipath. The setting was recited correctly and its absence was then described as harmless minutes later. The consequence — a hold-timer-length outage with every dashboard green — is the part to hold.
Recursion under variation. The single-overlay-drop case is now solid. A next hop reachable two ways, or a prefix learned from both hubs, has not been attempted.
